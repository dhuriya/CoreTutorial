*(Punctuated and organized by speaker and topic for reading practice. Panel: Host Tina Jha, with guests Mr. Manish Purohit — former ISRO scientist and Director of Nimbus Education — and Dr. Weniteshwar Sharma — former Deputy Director at ISRO.)*

## Introduction

**Tina Jha:** Namaskar, viewers. Hello, and welcome to Sunset TV. I am Tina Jha, you're watching Perspective.

Three years ago, on this day, India began a journey that would redefine its place in the history of space exploration. Chandrayaan-3 lifted off from the Satish Dhawan Space Center, carrying with it not just a spacecraft, but the aspirations of a nation determined to push the boundaries of science and exploration. Just weeks later, India made history with a successful soft landing near the moon's south polar region — the first nation ever to achieve this feat. It was a landmark moment that showcased ISRO's engineering excellence, scientific innovation, and India's growing stature as a leading space-faring nation.

But Chandrayaan-3's greatest legacy extends far beyond the lunar surface. This mission validated critical technologies, reinforced confidence in India's indigenous capabilities, and also provided fresh momentum to an ambitious future — one that includes missions like Gaganyaan, more advanced lunar missions in the future, a planned Bharatiya Antariksh Station, and deeper exploration of the solar system.

Three years on, Chandrayaan-3 is no longer just a remarkable achievement to celebrate, it is a milestone that continues to shape the direction of India's space program. So, where does India stand today? How did Chandrayaan-3 change ISRO's approach to future missions? Also, has it strengthened India's position in the global space ecosystem? And as the country pursues even more ambitious goals, what opportunities and challenges lie ahead?

To discuss these aspects, I'm joined by two distinguished guests. Let me begin the program by introducing them to you. I'm pleased to welcome Mr. Manish Purohit, former ISRO scientist and Director of Nimbus Education, and Dr. Weniteshwar Sharma, former Deputy Director at ISRO.

Gentlemen, thank you for taking the time and joining us for this program on Sunset TV.

## Chandrayaan-3's Place in ISRO's Journey

**Tina Jha:** Dr. Sharma, let me begin with your thoughts today, as we celebrate the third anniversary of Chandrayaan. Three years ago, when Chandrayaan lifted off on a mission which, of course, went on to make history — looking back, where would you place Chandrayaan-3 in the larger journey of India's space program?

**Dr. Sharma:** Namaskar to you and the million viewers of Sansad TV. It is a great opportunity to talk about ISRO and its achievements on this occasion, the completion of three years of Chandrayaan-3. It is really a great moment.

If you see the history of ISRO, 1962 is the beginning of ISRO, and it has completed 64 successful years of achievements in rockets, satellites, and various science programs. And if you see, in 1972, the first Aryabhata was made in three years. If you think of ISRO as a startup, within three years, Aryabhata was made and successfully launched, and even today there is no startup which has gone from scratch to launch in three years — that is the success story of ISRO, and Aryabhata has now completed 50 years of its existence.

Then we move on to 2008 — we had Chandrayaan-1, which found water on the moon. India was the first country, even though many countries had gone to the moon and landed astronauts on the moon, India had the pride of finding water on the moon in 2008 with Chandrayaan-1.

Then, in 2014, we had Mangalyaan, which was really a heartbreaking achievement — in terms of the finest technologies, the best of commercial efficiency, and, in the first attempt itself, going to Mars, which no other country had ever managed — that's another achievement you can recall.

And recently, in 2023, we had the successful launch of Chandrayaan-3, landing on the South Pole — the first time ever any country had gone to the moon's South Pole. Even Prime Minister Modi mentioned this achievement on his recent trip to Australia and New Zealand.

If you see these achievements, what we have learned in these three years, or in the past years, is that the culture of ISRO has propagated into the startups and also the industries that are making satellites and rockets. There are more than 550 industries working on high-tech satellites, rocket-making, various subsystems, and full-level rockets as well. Therefore, the ecosystem has completely changed — from only the government, only ISRO, to many, many industries and many, many startups coming into the arena of making satellites and rockets.

You could see that, at the beginning of the year, there was a launch of an Indian-made SAR satellite from the US, which successfully went into orbit, and we had a rocket made by startups which successfully went into space. Now, next month, you could observe that there are rockets that are going to be launched by startups as well.

**Tina Jha:** That's right. In recent years, we've seen greater collaboration between ISRO, the industry, academia, and, of course, the startups, as you mentioned. Also, ever since the launch of Chandrayaan-3 three years ago, we've seen several successful launches from ISRO. We'll be talking about how the success of the Chandrayaan mission boosted the confidence of our scientists and our engineers as well. But as we celebrate the third anniversary — Mr. Purohit, let me bring you in here.

## The Story of Resilience — From Chandrayaan-2's Setback to Chandrayaan-3's Success

**Tina Jha:** Let's also remember that every successful mission has a story behind it, and we clearly remember that Chandrayaan-3 came after the setback of Chandrayaan-2. So, if I may get your thoughts — you've also worked at ISRO — given your experience, and something that Dr. Sharma also highlighted, how do you think the Chandrayaan-3 mission specifically demonstrated the culture of resilience that is prevalent at ISRO, the ability of our scientists and engineers to stand back on their feet, learn from failure, and achieve what they've dreamt of?

**Mr. Purohit:** First, thank you, Tina, for giving me this opportunity to celebrate the third anniversary of the Chandrayaan-3 launch with you on Sansad TV.

If you talk about ISRO, ISRO's story of resilience and coming back from setbacks is huge. If you see, we have seen the trials of SLV, ASLV, then we had PSLV, and PSLV has been an amazing workhorse for India. The launch vehicle that lifted off with Chandrayaan, the LVM3, has a 100% success rate, and on top of it, recently I heard our chairman, Narayanan sir, state that LVM3 was developed in record time — it's kind of a world record for a space agency to develop such a full-fledged launch vehicle in record time with the given parameters.

So, ISRO has amazing stories, but if you specifically talk about Chandrayaan-3's soft landing on the moon, it was a transition from "can we do it" to "we can do world-class, very autonomously done space missions with full reliability." It was like we broke out of a psychological barrier. Previously it was, "can India do it? Will it be possible for India to do the soft landing on the moon?" There had been trials — in Chandrayaan-2 we tried, and then we failed. But then that psychological barrier, that institutional confidence, built up when we did Chandrayaan-3, and post-Chandrayaan-3, we have been on a perfect trajectory.

There have been a few setbacks — if you talk about the recent PSLV issues in the launch — but then we had SpaDeX. We have demonstrated that yes, we can do docking of two satellites in orbit, we can change the target, and then we can dock the two satellites — very critical technology for our upcoming missions. We have planned Chandrayaan-4, which will be a lunar sample return mission. We have a collaboration with Japan's space exploration agency for their lunar exploration program. We are building a much bigger Vikram to land their rover on the moon. So, big things are happening.

Recently, we are very close to having our own semi-cryogenic engine. So, something has completely changed. It's not just about ISRO, the story runs on two parallel tracks — one is with ISRO, and one is with the startups. The confidence that was built up in the startups, the founding members of that ecosystem — community funds started flowing in. Right now, we have a myriad of startups working on propulsion systems, working on reusable launch vehicles, working on CubeSats. Recently, we had the first-in-the-world OpticSAR satellite launched into orbit. Many things are happening right now in India, and I believe the transition point was when we successfully landed on the moon.

## Validated Technologies and Landing Capability

**Tina Jha:** That's right. I think, even for the common public, Dr. Sharma, the most defining image has to be the landing of Vikram on the moon — and, of course, not to forget the stature it gave us. We became the first country to attempt and succeed in a soft landing, especially in a part of the moon that many other countries had been trying to reach.

How did that boost the confidence of those associated with the project? Also, a related question — I was reading that Chandrayaan has validated critical landing technologies. So, how important, post-Chandrayaan, has that capability been, as we prepare for more and more complex planetary missions — something Mr. Purohit has already spoken about — but how has it enhanced our capability? I'd like to know your thoughts on that.

**Dr. Sharma:** It's an excellent question, thank you for asking such a nice question.

We learned a lot from Chandrayaan-2. From there, we improved the legs of the lander, we improved the software, we improved the entire electronic systems, we included mechanical systems, propulsion systems — the entire journey from Chandrayaan-2 to Chandrayaan-3, the learning was extremely high. Several models were made and thoroughly tested to ensure there wouldn't be any risks or failures possible in the Chandrayaan-3 landing.

Having said that, the last 56 minutes of Chandrayaan-3 were really breathtaking — from entering the moon's orbit, that 100-kilometer orbit, to the actual landing on the spot. This is really something — from the point of view of science, of technology, the type of sensors used, the type of actuators used, the software and algorithms — it was a real challenge, and the satisfaction it has given to the scientists and engineers of ISRO, and to the world at large, is extremely high.

We should remember one thing — any launch, any landing, any such mission, is not just for India, it is for the entire world to observe and for the entire world to learn from. It's mutual — it's not that only they learn from us, we also learn from every mission of every country, and that is one very big and major takeaway from that landing, which was thought to be impossible — landing on the South Pole, where India demonstrated that we could successfully land for the first time, without any error and with zero margin, at 6:02, exactly on August 23rd. That is a real achievement for scientists and engineers.

Any such launch is not limited to only engineers and scientists and industries. Even the industries — there were 157 industries which worked on Chandrayaan-3 — really gained, from the point of view of their policy systems, their confidence, and their pride of contributing to such a mission — something we should really appreciate. That experience will pave the way for many more such missions to come.

The second point is the most important thing — the 140 crore people of this country were really glued to the TV, praying for the successful landing of Chandrayaan-3. I don't think there's anything else — cricket matches, football matches — where the entire country was united like this. 140 crore people were united to see this successful landing, and the entire country celebrated. That is a very big takeaway, along with the motivation it gave to students to study science, technology, and rocket science — the confidence they get from this is something we should take as a very big positive.

## Global Perception of India's Space Capabilities

**Tina Jha:** Absolutely, absolutely. There's no doubt the success of Chandrayaan-3 brought immense pride to every Indian, not just within the country but abroad as well. What was also interesting was that it was celebrated across the world — in recent years, we've seen Chandrayaan receive top awards, and it's been mentioned by different space agencies as well.

So, can we say, Mr. Purohit, that India is now no longer seen as only a cost-effective space nation, but a nation that is increasingly becoming capable of executing highly complex missions? I'm not saying we didn't have space partnerships before — we did have many — but post-Chandrayaan-3, do you think the world's perception, especially of the leading space agencies across the world, has changed about India's space capabilities?

**Mr. Purohit:** Yeah, that's for sure, that has happened. See, actually, if you look at the era before Chandrayaan-3 and the era after it, the main difference is what ISRO is right now aspiring for.

We have parallel tracks running right now from ISRO — we are validating our upgraded cryogenic engine, and that's ready for flight, we are finalizing our semi-cryogenic engine, we have already done the critical test for our Gaganyaan mission, and we have open collaborations for Gaganyaan with different ground stations globally. If you talk about the mission right after Chandrayaan, Aditya-L1, that was again a global collaboration — we had a lot of support from ESA, the European Space Agency, for that mission.

So, right now, if we place India — obviously, we are the nation that has soft-landed on the moon, and we carry that kind of pride with us, that's one thing. The other thing you talked about, perception — it has completely and totally changed. Right now, collaborations are open. If you talk about startups, I interact a lot with startups, and startups from European countries are ready to collaborate with the startups being nurtured by India. There are collaborations for startups in Australia and New Zealand with Indian startups too.

So, it's not just that India is moving forward in the space domain, it's like the whole global community is moving, and we are right now part of it. The thrust is towards the complete global space economy, and we are a big part of it right now — that's what we aspired for when India adopted the new space policy. We wanted to have a nice chunk of that billion-dollar industry, and that's what we are on track to achieve.

We have recently privatized our PSLV, we are in talks to transfer the technology for our very reliable LVM3 as well. This means we are thinking beyond those boundaries where ISRO was only responsible for developing and designing the launch vehicle, designing the satellites. ISRO right now is talking about more complex challenges — the Gaganyaan mission, the Venus orbiter mission, the moon lander mission, an Indian-on-the-moon mission, a more powerful next-generation launch vehicle, a reusable launch vehicle — so many things. We are transitioning from just ISRO to a big space economy, where there are around 300-plus startups and enterprises coming together and building the whole space story of India.

## Inspiring the Next Generation

**Tina Jha:** That's right, the next decade does promise to be one of the most exciting phases in India's space journey. Before we proceed — a point Dr. Sharma mentioned, about the Chandrayaan mission playing an important role in inspiring youngsters to pursue science, engineering, innovation, and some interest in space science as well.

Mr. Purohit, I would like to know your thoughts — how important do you think this kind of sustained public awareness and enthusiasm is for the future of India's space program? As you rightly pointed out, the next few years are lined up with one important mission after another.

**Mr. Purohit:** See, actually, if you talk about the present scenario, the fervor and zeal that was inculcated in the youth when we talk about our soft landing on the moon — it brought the whole country together. It was like we won the World Cup, and we're celebrating — but it was much bigger than that. We won a big challenge of landing softly on the moon, and India won. That was the kind of feeling for the general public, who aren't able to make much out of the space technology itself, but they believe that yes, India has done something immensely great.

Now, think along those lines — the general public believes this is something really big, and that trickles down to the next generation, that this contributes back to the country. So, when the next generation comes up, they aspire to be part of that ecosystem, and that's what's happening right now. Many, many students have switched from thinking about just being a computer science engineer or an electronics engineer, to wanting to be in the field of space. They want to go into the aerospace industry, they're searching for options in aerospace, they're picking degrees and selecting colleges specifically where they can nurture more skills in the aerospace domain, because they want to contribute more to this side.

When that starts happening for a country, you have a whole generation ready to contribute, and when you have skilled and powerful, talented young minds with you, then what you see is what's happening in India right now. Startups are rolling out — it doesn't matter whether it's an IIT or any tier-1, tier-2, or tier-3 college, whether it's a metropolitan city or a small town — startups are rolling out, and they are at a technology readiness level of four, five, six, where they are ready to take seed funds. ISRO has opened the doors, they have put in the funds which help them nurture initially, there is hand-holding from ISRO to nurture those startups. So, that fervor has not yet died down, and probably the next decade is ours.

## Challenges Ahead

**Tina Jha:** That's true, and we are particularly excited about the ambitious Gaganyaan mission — we've already seen rigorous preparation underway by the relevant stakeholders. Dr. Sharma, as we pursue more and more ambitious goals for our space missions, let's also talk about some of the biggest challenges we have to overcome in the next few years.

**Dr. Sharma:** Definitely, there are many challenges — in terms of technology, in terms of availability of critical components, in terms of availability of many materials, and so on, something the country has to own or make available itself.

The second thing is that the industries which are doing many of these rocket parts, satellite parts, and subsystems have to graduate themselves much more, to invest much more into the realization of the entire rocket, such as the PSLV, LVM3, or even the GSLV launch vehicles. Industry contribution, industry confidence in doing such rockets, and making it commercially viable — that's something the country really has to look into.

The third and most important thing is that the International Space Station is coming to its end by 2030. Therefore, there is a need for collaboration to build what is called the Bharatiya Antariksh Station, which Prime Minister Narendra Modi has very nicely defined and declared will be operational by 2035 to 2040. For that, wherever Prime Minister Modi is traveling, there is talk about the space program, space activities, and cooperation — including for the next replacement of the ISS, where ISRO is looking to be a partner.

So, the biggest challenge is that we need proper cooperation with all the other space agencies, which the government is very effectively pursuing, and also getting to the "Atmanirbhar" — self-reliant — status in every aspect of rocketry, satellite-making, and so on, so that we don't have to depend on others for self-sustenance and self-governance. Of course, cooperation is also required, because space is something everyone learns from each other, and that's something we have to work on.

More importantly, we have to keep going — we had two setbacks recently, and therefore there is some sort of inertia that has built up over the last five, six months, and that inertia we necessarily have to get over. It's not that this is something new — there have been failures before, we have gotten over them and moved on, and we have achieved many milestones. Now is the time to move on, have many more PSLV launches, many more GSLV launches, and give confidence to this country and the world that any failure will never be a lasting setback, and we should necessarily march forward.

Gaganyaan is another major challenge, one that has been taking time in terms of testing and getting the various subsystems and systems made. Gaganyaan also has to get into orbit by 2027 at the earliest, and the astronauts have to be in orbit by at least 2028. That will give us the impetus for the human spaceflight program — human spaceflight definitely has to get to the next level of realization, because if we ever want our own BSS, our human spaceflight program has to necessarily be realized.

## The Biggest Test of India's Capabilities

**Tina Jha:** We've seen visible efforts undertaken — by the government, by ISRO, by space scientists, and, of course, by those leading the innovation game in the sector. The most important point here is that, in recent years, that kind of ecosystem has been built and strengthened, which will help us realize these goals. There are certain challenges that we did discuss with Dr. Sharma, which we need to tackle in time.

But as we move towards these ambitious goals, Mr. Purohit, one last question for you — which of these do you think will test India's capabilities the most?

**Mr. Purohit:** See, right now, if you talk about the challenges, the one big challenge in front of us is our launch cadence. We are launching very few satellites. I've heard someone talking about how internal demand has to come up, and that we should be launching a minimum of, say, 10 to 15 satellites every year. We have startups, we have the promise that we can build satellites, we can build launch vehicles, and the startups are really working hard, ISRO is planning some bigger missions — but still, the next big thing we have to tackle is what China has recently done. China has demonstrated that they can bring back the first stage of the rocket and catch it with a wire net. SpaceX demonstrated that a decade back, so that's the worst-case scenario right now.

It's not that India isn't working on that — ISRO is also working on reusable launch vehicles. Initially, it was called RLV, but I think right now it's referred to as VTVL, vertical takeoff and vertical landing launch vehicle. So, it's not that we're not working on it, but if you look at the global scenario, all of a sudden you see China is ramping up the number of launches every year — there's a huge internal demand there, they're building constellations. We are reliable, we are very cost-effective, but when it comes to lifting capabilities, how much weight, how much mass you can lift with your launch vehicle, and whether it's reusable — because reusability brings the cost dramatically down — this is the challenge, right now, that I see we have to tackle.

Our launch vehicles — PSLV, GSLV, and LVM3 — have a limitation on how much they can carry. If we have a bigger satellite, a heavier payload, we have to go to SpaceX for help, or maybe to Arianespace, as we have done previously, when we didn't have GSLV and LVM3 for our heavier satellites. So, this is something ISRO, or maybe India, has to tackle.

Second, when you talk about startups — they surely need a lot of hand-holding, from experts, from the community, from ISRO's side. I know there's a huge push from the government's side to nurture these startups, but there's still a very fine line between what we call being ambitious and being practical. That's kind of a tussle that's right now happening in the ecosystem.
